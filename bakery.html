Four Hundred Degrees

One fire, never allowed to go cold

The stone oven was built in 1996 from firebrick and Aswan clay, and it has been burning citrus and olive wood ever since. It takes two hours to come up to temperature and gives us bread with a blistered crust no gas oven has ever matched.

What We Bake

Six counters, one standard

Everything below leaves the oven and goes straight to the counter. Nothing is held past the day it was baked — what is left at close goes to the neighbourhood, as it always has.

Fresh Bread

Baladi, aish shamsi and semit. Wholemeal, bran-dusted, puffed on bare stone — and never more than twenty minutes old when it reaches you.

Croissants

Seventy-two hours of cold fermentation, three folds of French butter, baked hourly from six. When they are gone, they are gone.

Feteer

Thirty hand-thrown layers with clarified butter between each one. Sweet with honey and cream, or stuffed with rumi cheese and basterma.

Pastries

Pistachio and orange-blossom danish, date maamoul pressed in olive-wood moulds, and a gold box of twelve petit fours.

Egyptian Baked Goods

Kahk el-Eid filled with agameya and walnut, betifour, and hawawshi pressed straight against the oven wall until it blisters.

Semit & Rings

Boiled, then baked, then rolled in sesame while still wet. Crisp outside, soft within — the street-corner classic, done properly.

Order from the Bakery

The Room at Dawn

Warm flour, cold air,
and the first tray out

The bakery counter opens at half past five, an hour and a half before the dining room. For that ninety minutes the place belongs to taxi drivers, night nurses and anyone else who knows that the best bread of the day is the first.

Come early. Stand at the counter with a semit and a glass of tea, watch Amira work the bench, and understand why we have never once considered buying a bread supplier.
